Checking your blood pressure at home for a week

If your doctor or nurse asked you to check your blood pressure at home for a week, this page explains how the published guidelines say to do it: how many readings, when, how to sit, why the first day is often left out, and how to work out an average. It ends with a free printable log sheet you can fill in with a pen.

Your own doctor's instructions come first. If they told you something different from this page, do what they said. This page explains published guidance. It is not medical advice and does not tell you what your numbers mean.

Get emergency care now (call 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK) if you have chest pain, trouble breathing, or signs of a stroke such as weakness, numbness, a change in vision or trouble speaking. Do not wait to take another reading.

Why a week at home

A reading in a clinic is one moment, in a setting that can push the numbers up or down. Guidelines from the UK, the US and Canada all accept a series of home readings as a way to see your usual blood pressure outside the clinic. The UK's NICE guideline uses home readings to confirm a diagnosis when 24-hour ambulatory monitoring is not suitable, and the American Heart Association recommends home monitoring for people with high blood pressure so their care team can see whether treatment is working. Sources are listed at the end.

The week at a glance

  • Two sessions a day: one in the morning and one in the evening.
  • Two readings each session, about one minute apart. Write both down.
  • Seven days in a row. That makes 28 readings in total.
  • Day 1 is usually left out of the average, and the readings from days 2 to 7 are averaged.
  • Sit quietly for five minutes before each session, back supported, feet flat, arm resting at heart level, cuff on bare skin.

Before you start

The monitor

  • The American Heart Association recommends an automatic monitor with a cuff that goes around your upper arm. It says wrist and finger monitors give less reliable readings.
  • Choose a monitor that has been validated (independently tested for accuracy). Lists of validated monitors: validatebp.org (US), Hypertension Canada's recommended device list (Canada) and the British and Irish Hypertension Society website (UK, named by NICE).
  • The cuff must fit your arm. Measure around your upper arm and check it against the size printed on the cuff. Both NICE and the AHA stress the right cuff size; the AHA says a cuff of the wrong size gives an inaccurate reading.
  • If you can, take the monitor to your next appointment so your care team can check you are using it correctly. The AHA suggests this.

How to take each reading

These steps come from the American Heart Association's home monitoring page, with the sitting position also described by NICE and by Hypertension Canada's guideline program.

  1. Plan the 30 minutes before. No smoking, no caffeinated drinks and no exercise in the 30 minutes before you measure. Empty your bladder.
  2. Bare arm. Roll up or take off the sleeve. The cuff goes on bare skin, not over clothing. Either arm is fine; the AHA says there is usually little difference between them. Use the same arm all week and note which one.
  3. Sit correctly. Sit in a chair with your back supported, legs uncrossed and feet flat on the floor. Rest your arm on a table so the middle of the cuff is at the level of your heart. The bottom edge of the cuff sits just above the bend of your elbow.
  4. Rest for five minutes. Sit still and quiet for at least five minutes before you press start. Do not talk or use your phone, during the rest or the reading.
  5. Take two readings, about one minute apart. Stay seated between them.
  6. Write both down straight away: the top number (systolic), the bottom number (diastolic), the pulse if your monitor shows it, and the time.

Tip: measure at about the same times each day, for example before breakfast and before your evening meal. The AHA recommends the same time every day. If you take blood pressure medicine, ask your doctor whether to measure before or after your dose.

Why the first day is often left out

Both NICE and Hypertension Canada's guideline program say to throw away the first day's readings and average the rest. You still take and write down the day 1 readings; they just do not go into the average unless your doctor asks for them.

Working out your average

You can do this with a calculator, or leave it for your doctor. Work out the top and bottom numbers separately.

  1. Add up every top (systolic) number from days 2 to 7. With 4 readings a day for 6 days that is 24 numbers.
  2. Divide the total by how many numbers you added. That is your average systolic.
  3. Do the same with the bottom (diastolic) numbers.
  4. Write the average as top over bottom, for example 128/79.

If you missed a session, average the readings you have and divide by that smaller count. Do not make up a missing reading. Bring the whole sheet to your appointment, not only the average, so your doctor can see the pattern and any gaps.

Your doctor decides what the average means for you. This page does not interpret numbers.

If one reading is very high

The American Heart Association says a single high reading is not an immediate cause for alarm: take it again and write down both. If readings are suddenly above 180/120 mm Hg, it advises waiting at least a minute and measuring again, and contacting your healthcare professional straight away if they are still that high. If a reading is above 180/120 and you have symptoms such as chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness, weakness, a change in vision or difficulty speaking, it advises calling 911 without waiting to see whether the number comes down.

The core routine is the same everywhere: seated, rested, two readings a session, morning and evening, written down. The differences are in how many days count and whether day 1 is used.
